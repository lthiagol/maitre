The Sous-Chef tab — archived designs

Nine designs that were not taken forward, kept whole. They are here because the reasoning behind them is still worth having — and because a design that was rejected once may be the right answer to a different question later.

The live design is the ticket — sous-chef-mockups.html, which carries a simulator: the harness, the crew, the sources, the budget and the phase are all pickable, and the tab redraws itself.

The tab's fixed ground — the tab is Sous-Chef, between Board and Log (Q16, Q17). Its move is it starts the automation: space spawns the crew (Q18). The rows carry the glossary's job names — orchestrator, runner, reviewer.

The four required controls — harness (opencode · pi · custom) · crew (1 · 2 · 3, default 3) · tasks (recipes or backlog items, from Ready) · status (who is working, and how many execution/review interactions have happened).

1The panel

A sectioned form. Four blocks, each a selector, the status at the bottom. The tab is a control panel you set up once, then watch.

Makes easy

Every control is visible at once, in the order the chef sets them up. Nothing is hidden behind a mode, and the composition under each count is spelled out — so "2" is never a bare number.

Costs

The status is a footnote under a form. Three sous-chefs read as three lines of text, not as agents — the tab never shows who is doing what. And it is a second form tab, next to Settings.

2The crew

A roster. One row per sous-chef, carrying its role, its harness, its state and its current work. The tab's list is the status; the selectors sit in a strip above it.

Makes easy

"Who is working now, on what, and for how long" is the first thing on screen — the chef never hunts for it. Selecting a row puts a sous-chef under the cursor, so the detail pane has something to show.

Costs

The three selectors are demoted to a strip, so changing the crew mid-run is less obvious than it should be. And a roster of agents is a new kind of list for this console — every other list holds plan items.

3The round

The automation as a tree, in the Board's own language. The crew hangs off the round it is working on, and the queue hangs off the round that is next.

 ⋈ maitre · kb
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Home    Recipes    Backlog    Ready    Board  ▸ Sous-Chef    Log    Settings
 opencode · 3 sous-chefs · ready (4) · round 2 of 3 · interactions 6 of 12
 ROUND 2 · RCP04
 ├─ orchestrator        working   routing the round · asked once
 ├─ runner              working   BL05 · 2 of 3 criteria proven
 └─ reviewer            idle      waiting on the runner
 QUEUE
 ├─ RCP02  Sign off the menu              queued
 ├─ RCP03  Wire the review queue          queued
 └─ BL05   Wire the review queue          next

Makes easy

It reuses a language the console already speaks, so the tab needs no new vocabulary. The crew visibly belongs to a round, and the queue visibly belongs to the next one — the "interactions" count is the tree's depth, not a number in a corner.

Costs

A tree is a picture of a shape, and a shape is static — it cannot show a blocked agent asking a question, or a harness that changed mid-run. It is also the least direct way to change a setting.

4The feed

A live journal of the automation, like the Log tab. The status is the last event, and the counters sit in the summary.

 16:43  runner        BL05 · 2 of 3 criteria proven
 16:41  orchestrator  routed RCP04 to the runner
 16:40  runner        started BL05
 16:38  reviewer      RCP04 reviewed · changes needed
 16:37  runner        RCP04 implemented · 3 of 3 proven
 16:35  orchestrator  round 1 of 3 opened
 16:33  orchestrator  crew started · opencode · 3 sous-chefs

Makes easy

The audit trail is the status: who did what, in what order, and how many interactions it took. A blocked agent's question is visible where it happened, not in a summary. Nothing here is invented — it is the journal.

Costs

It answers "what happened" and never "what is happening now" — the chef reads the last line to learn the crew is idle. And the Log tab already does exactly this for the plan, so the tab risks being a second Log.

Makes easy

Nothing is hidden and nothing needs scrolling — the whole configuration and the whole state at once, which is what an operator wants. The ASKED block gives a blocked agent's question a home, which is where the chef's attention is actually needed (Q18).

Costs

Two columns in 80 columns is 38 each, and the left column is a form of short labels — it reads like a settings sheet. The busiest thing on the tab, the live state, gets the smaller half of the screen on the right.

7The bench

The crew as columns. The composition is not a number in a list — it is the shape of the screen. Three sous-chefs, three columns. Two, two. One, one column and a note that the chef reviews.

Makes easy

The strongest version of "the count is a composition". Choosing 2 makes the orchestrator's column grow to hold both its jobs and the reviewer's; choosing 1 leaves one column and says the chef holds the review. The chef sees the crew's shape before starting it.

Costs

Three columns in 80 columns is 26 each — too narrow for "waiting on the runner" without wrapping. It wants the split layout at 160, so the tab would have two different shapes, and the narrow one would be the one most chefs use. Also, the columns are a picture of a moment: a blocked agent has nowhere to put its question.

8The booth

A one-screen control booth. The crew's live state is a band at the top, the configuration a strip under it, and a pinned ASKED block at the foot that only earns space when an agent is actually asking.

From the deck (5) — the state moves to the top, the form shrinks, and the question gets its own room.

Makes easy

The live state is the first thing on the tab, always, and the vital signs ride with it — the chef never has to ask whether the automation is speaking the plan's rules. The configuration is a strip, because it is set once. And the ASKED block is the only thing that grows, so the chef's attention lands where it is needed.

Costs

The configuration is compressed to the point of being hard to change — three selectors on one line. And "answer" puts the chef in the middle of a conversation between two agents, which is a move this tab may not want: Q18 gives that job to the orchestrator.

9The hour

The panel turned upside down. The tab opens with now — the round, the crew, the counters, the vital signs — and the configuration sits below it, for when the chef wants to change something.

From the panel (1) — the status moves from the foot to the head, and the form follows.

Makes easy

The tab's first line answers the chef's first question — "what is happening?" — and never stops answering it. Configuration is explicitly the thing you scroll down to, which is the honest relationship between the two: one is watched, one is set.

Costs

Two bands on one screen is a compromise the console has never had to make elsewhere — every other tab is one thing. And a tab that can be scrolled has a state the chef has to remember.

10The table

The crew as data. One row per sous-chef, one column per fact — the console's own house style, the way a recipe row is a table. The configuration is a single strip above it.

From the deck (5) — the live column gives up its column and becomes a full-width table.

Makes easy

Nothing is prose, so nothing is ambiguous: the chef can see at a glance that the reviewer is idle and the runner is not, that everyone is on the same harness, and that the round is 2 of 3. A table is also the easiest thing in this console to extend — a new column is a new fact, not a new layout.

Costs

Tables are for comparing things of the same kind, and a sous-chef is not a plan item — the tab would be the first in the console to list something that is not in the plan. It also has no room for a blocked agent's question, which is the one thing on this tab that is not tabular.
